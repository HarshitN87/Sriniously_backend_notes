Chapter the First

How Requests Travel on the Internet

"A chronological, physical, and mythological account of the long, perilous journey of a compressed heart—from the mechanical capture of a touch to the deep-sea cable crossing, the three-way handshake, and the final server gate."
♦ ✦ ♦

Consider the epic flight of Hanuman across the roaring ocean. Bound for the fortress island of Lanka to deliver a message of hope to the captive Sita, Hanuman stands upon the peaks of Mount Mahendra, gathers his life energy, and leaps into the sky. He does not travel as a weightless spirit; he is a physical entity of immense power carrying a high cryptographic payload: the golden signet ring, the Mudrika, inscribed with the royal name of Sri Rama. This ring is no mere ornament; it is a cryptographically signed token of authenticity, verifying his identity beyond doubt to the receiver.

The sky Hanuman navigates is not a quiet void. It is a hostile, noisy channel, thick with atmospheric turbulence and guarded by terrible demonic adversaries. First rises Surasa, the mother of serpents, who demands that Hanuman enter her mouth. This is the ancient protocol of size negotiation and Maximum Transmission Unit (MTU) matching. Surasa expands her throat to a width of ten leagues; Hanuman doubles his size to twenty. She expands to forty; he swells to eighty. When her jaw stretches to a massive hundred leagues, Hanuman executes a brilliant dynamic sizing maneuver: he instantly shrinks to the size of a thumb, slips through her cavernous throat, and exits before her sluggish reflexes can snap her jaws shut.

In the world of networks, this size-negotiation mirrors the process of Path MTU Discovery (PMTUD). Every physical network link along a route has a Maximum Transmission Unit—the largest packet size it can transmit without fragmentation. A standard Ethernet link has an MTU of 1500 bytes. However, tunneling protocols, VPNs, or legacy networks may restrict this limit. When a packet exceeding this limit arrives at a router with the "Don't Fragment" (DF) bit set, the router has no choice but to drop it and return an ICMP Type 3 Code 4 message: "Destination Unreachable, Fragmentation Needed and DF Set." Like Hanuman matching Surasa's jaw, the sending host must dynamically adjust its segment sizes to fit the narrowest bottleneck on the path, ensuring the packet flows smoothly without being torn to fragments.

Next, Hanuman is dragged downward by Sinhika, the shadow-grabbing demon. Sinhika does not attack his body directly; she catches his reflection on the water below, pulling his shadow to arrest his motion. This is the mythological equivalent of network sniffing, traffic analysis, and session hijacking. By grabbing the metadata shadow, she threatens to intercept and destroy the packet. An attacker sitting passively on a fiber trunk or wireless access point does not block your signal; they mirror it, capturing the packet shadows to execute offline decryption, session extraction, or timing analysis. Hanuman must dive deep, penetrate her vulnerabilities, and destroy her from within to protect the integrity of his mission. When Hanuman finally reaches Sita, verifies her identity, and delivers the Mudrika, he does not simply vanish. He returns across the ocean carrying her hair jewel, the Chudamani, which acts as the perfect, authenticated Acknowledgment (ACK) packet, completing the trust loop and assuring Rama that the message was received.

This is the central truth of communications engineering: Human intent is rich, multi-dimensional, and highly complex; the physical universe is noisy, local, and deeply indifferent. When we tap a screen, we treat the action as telepathy. We assume the request simply teleports from our phone to a database. But bytes do not float, and they certainly do not teleport. Every click, every scroll, and every transaction is a physical event slung across a hostile universe.

On land, our digital messages travel in ways reminiscent of Ashoka’s Harkara runners on the ancient Uttarapatha highway. In the Mauryan Empire, royal decrees were written on dried palm-leaf documents, sealed in leather cases, and slung across the shoulders of relay runners. A runner would sprint at maximum speed from one rest house (a chotti or sara) to the next. At the boundary, the exhausting runner handed the scroll to a fresh runner, who would carry it the next hop. This is the historical antecedent of packet-switched routing and hop-by-hop forwarding.

To survive this journey, your request must be packaged, addressed, secured, routed, and verified under a stack of protocols representing over fifty years of continuous coordination failures, brilliant workarounds, and deep mathematical insights. Let us trace that journey, step by step, from the moment your finger touches the glass to the moment the vault doors of the database slide shut.

1. The Genesis: The Click Event and the Micro-Physics of Touch

Let us begin at the absolute boundary of the physical and the virtual: the capacitive glass of the screen on your mobile telephone. The screen is not a passive pane of glass; it is a multi-layered matrix of electrical sensors. It is coated with an incredibly thin, transparent layer of indium tin oxide (ITO), arranged in a grid of microscopic coordinate lines. A tiny electric charge is run through this grid, creating a uniform electrostatic field across the face of the device.

Your body is an electrical conductor. When your thumb—which consists of carbon, water, and salt—approaches the screen, it acts as an electrical capacitor. Your finger draws a microscopic amount of charge from the grid, causing a localized drop in capacitance at the precise coordinates of your touch. A dedicated hardware chip—the touchscreen controller—constantly scans this grid, hundreds of times per second. It registers the drop in capacitance, translates it into numerical (X, Y) coordinate variables, and triggers a hardware interrupt. This interrupt is sent straight to the operating system kernel.

The physics of capacitive touch can be categorized into two primary methodologies: self-capacitance and mutual-capacitance. In self-capacitance systems, the controller measures an entire row or column of the ITO grid against the ground. While simple, self-capacitance suffers from "ghosting" when multiple fingers touch the screen simultaneously, as the intersection points become ambiguous. Modern smartphones utilize mutual-capacitance grids. In a mutual-capacitance system, every intersection of row and column lines forms a capacitor. The touchscreen controller drives an AC voltage through the drive lines and measures the charge received on the sensing lines. When your finger touches the screen, it alters the local mutual capacitance at that specific intersection because the human body provides an alternate path for the electric field lines, diverting some charge. This allows the controller to resolve dozens of simultaneous touch coordinates with pinpoint accuracy.

The capacitance of a parallel plate capacitor is mathematically defined by:

\[C = \frac{\epsilon A}{d}\]
where \(\epsilon\) represents the permittivity of the dielectric layer, \(A\) represents the area of the overlapping plates, and \(d\) is the distance between them. When a human finger approaches, it introduces a secondary grounded path, altering the electrical field lines and causing a measurable shift in the capacitance values. Once these analog voltage drops are registered, they are digitized by an Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) inside the controller. The controller translates these voltage changes into discrete spatial coordinates. It then raises a hardware interrupt by driving a signal high on the CPU's Interrupt Request (IRQ) line. The CPU, which might be in the middle of executing a rendering loop or compiling background code, must halt immediately.

When the IRQ line is asserted, the CPU pauses its current execution pipeline, saves its Program Counter (PC) and registers to the kernel stack, and jumps to a specific memory address stored in the Interrupt Vector Table (IVT). This address points to the operating system's touchscreen driver. The driver reads the digitizer's registers over a hardware bus—typically using the I2C (Inter-Integrated Circuit) or SPI (Serial Peripheral Interface) protocol—to retrieve the raw coordinate packet.

The operating system kernel wraps these coordinates in a generic input event and pushes it to the OS input queue. The window manager process (such as Wayland on Linux, Quartz on iOS, or Android's InputDispatcher) pops the event, determines which application window currently holds focus, and dispatches the event via an Inter-Process Communication (IPC) socket to the browser process.

Inside the browser process, this coordinate is converted into a software event. The browser’s event-loop fires a "hit test," calculating which element on the page lives at the coordinates (X, Y). It finds the heart-shaped icon. It places a task in the browser's microtask queue.

The browser's JavaScript engine (V8 or JavaScriptCore) picks up the task from the queue. It executes a callback function associated with the click event. The code does not directly turn the heart red. If it did, and the network failed, the interface would lie to you—it would show a successful action that the database never recorded. Instead, the application must construct a formal letter—an HTTP request—and ask the server for permission.

The JavaScript runtime invokes the network API (such as fetch() or XMLHttpRequest). It passes a target address, the resource address, specifies the active verb, POST, and appends metadata headers and a JSON payload containing your user ID and action state.

Consider the mechanical archaeology of this message. Every single line in this HTTP header is separated by a specific character combination: the resource address, or CRLF (Carriage Return + Line Feed). Why? Because the internet’s most modern protocols still carry the ghosts of 1960s mechanical teletype machines. On an old mechanical typewriter or teletype, when a line of text was finished, the machine had to perform two distinct physical actions: first, the heavy metal print head had to be slid all the way back to the left margin (the Carriage Return, the resource address), and second, the rubber cylinder had to rotate to advance the paper up by one line (the Line Feed, the resource address). If you only performed a Carriage Return, you would write over the line you just typed, creating an illegible ink smudge.

When early web architects built the HTTP protocol in 1991, they wanted it to be simple and text-based, so that standard terminal emulators could read and write it natively. They cemented this teletype carriage-and-advance sequence directly into the structural carbon-steel of the protocol. Today, billions of requests passing through fiber cables at the speed of light still pause after every line to pretend they are mechanical typewriter heads returning to column zero. The request is now constructed in the phone's memory. It has an address: api.instagram.com. But this name is useless to the physical hardware of routers and switches. Routers do not speak English; they do not understand names, companies, or brands. They only understand IP addresses—long, numeric coordinates like 157.240.1.35. We must find the numbers.

2. DNS Resolution: The Cartographer's Dilemma

In the earliest days of ARPANET (the precursor to the internet), translating a human-readable name into a network address was simple. The network was small—a few hundred research computers. A single librarian named Elizabeth Feinler at the Stanford Research Institute maintained a single master file called HOSTS.TXT. Every computer on the network would periodically log in to Stanford's server via FTP, download this flat file, and save it locally. If a new node joined the network, they phoned Stanford, spoke to Elizabeth, and she typed the new record into the ledger.

By 1983, this centralization collapsed. The network was growing exponentially. The bandwidth consumed by thousands of computers downloading the massive, ever-expanding HOSTS.TXT file was saturating the network lines. If a server address changed, it took days for the change to propagate down to local computers. In 1983, Paul Mockapetris designed a brilliant, decentralized, hierarchical database called the Domain Name System (DNS), formalized in RFC 882 and 883. DNS did away with the master list. Instead, it organized the domain namespace into a tree structure, managed by a hierarchy of authoritative servers.

When your phone needs to resolve the name api.instagram.com, it initiates a multi-layered search. It first looks inside its own local caches. It checks the browser DNS cache, which maintains a fast memory cache of recent lookups. If empty, it queries the operating system cache. The OS checks its own local caches and consults the local /etc/hosts file. If empty, the phone fires a UDP packet to your home router, which holds its own cache of domain name entries.

If all local caches fail, the lookup must leave your house and head to your Internet Service Provider's Recursive Resolver. A recursive resolver is like an ambitious, tireless clerk. When a resolver receives a query for api.instagram.com and does not have the answer cached, it executes an iterative lookup on your behalf.

First, the recursive resolver queries a Root Name Server. There are exactly 13 Root Server IP addresses in the world, labeled A through M. This limitation is a direct result of the physical limits of network packets in 1983. A standard DNS query travels over UDP (User Datagram Protocol), which limits the payload size of a single unfragmented packet to 512 bytes to ensure it can travel through any primitive network line without getting chopped up. To fit all Root Server addresses, their names, and their metadata inside a single 512-byte packet, Mockapetris could pack at most 13 server entries. While there are only 13 IP addresses, today those addresses are backed by over a thousand physical, load-balanced servers globally using Anycast routing—but the logical limit of 13 remains frozen in stone.

The Root Server does not know where Instagram lives. Instead, it reads the rightmost segment of your address—the Top-Level Domain (TLD)—which is .com. The Root Server replies: "I do not know the address of api.instagram.com. However, I know where the Top-Level Domain registrars for .com reside. Here is their address."

The recursive resolver receives this card, thanks the Root Server, and immediately fires a new query to the TLD Name Server (managed by Verisign for .com domains). The TLD server inspects the next level down—instagram.com. It replies: "I do not know the IP of api.instagram.com. But I know that the authoritative server for Instagram's domains is managed by Route 53 (or Instagram’s own network). Here is the IP of Instagram’s Authoritative Name Server."

Finally, the resolver queries the Authoritative Name Server. This server is the ultimate source of truth. It belongs to Instagram, and it holds the definitive zone files. It reads the full subdomain: api.instagram.com. It responds with the final prize: the IPv4 address 157.240.1.35. The resolver saves this address in its local cache and returns the IP address to your phone.

Plate II: The Hierarchy of the Domain Name System

In the DNS history, security was long treated as an afterthought. DNS queries travel as unencrypted plain text. In 2008, security researcher Dan Kaminsky discovered a devastating vulnerability in DNS resolvers: DNS Cache Poisoning. Because DNS relies on UDP, a malicious actor could intercept a resolver’s outgoing query and spoof the response, returning their own malicious IP before the actual authoritative server could reply.

The Kaminsky attack exploited a structural flaw in DNS resolver lookup loops. When a recursive resolver needs to resolve a name like api.instagram.com, it sends an outgoing query with a 16-bit transaction ID. The resolver must match the incoming response's Transaction ID with its outstanding query. If an attacker throws thousands of spoofed responses at the resolver simultaneously, each containing a different guessed Transaction ID, they have a high probability of guessing the correct ID before the real authoritative server responds.

Even worse, if the resolver queried nonexistent123.instagram.com, it would receive a response from Instagram's authoritative server indicating that the record does not exist. However, the attacker could forge a response indicating that nonexistent123.instagram.com does not exist, but in the "Authority" section of the DNS packet, they could append an NS (Name Server) record asserting that the authoritative server for the entire instagram.com domain had moved to an IP address controlled by the attacker. Once the resolver accepted this response, its cache was poisoned. Any subsequent lookup for api.instagram.com or secure-login.instagram.com would bypass the real server entirely and query the attacker's server, directing thousands of users to a malicious phishing site.

The immediate fix was Source Port Randomization, multiplying the complexity of the guess by an additional 16 bits. Instead of using a fixed outgoing port (such as 53), the resolver randomizes the source port across a range of available high-numbered ports (e.g., 49152 to 65535). This forces the attacker to guess both the Transaction ID and the exact source port simultaneously, elevating the search space from \(2^{16}\) combinations to \(2^{32}\) combinations, making brute-force poisoning within the narrow response window mathematically impractical.

Later, the industry formalized DNSSEC (Domain Name System Security Extensions), which introduces cryptographic signatures to DNS records. With DNSSEC, every zone file is cryptographically signed using public-key cryptography, ensuring that resolvers can verify that the IP they received has not been tampered with or forged in transit. DNSSEC establishes a rigid cryptographic chain of trust down the hierarchical tree. Each parent zone signs the public key of its child zone using a Delegation Signer (DS) record. The Root Zone itself is signed using a highly secure Key Signing Key (KSK), whose public key is hardcoded directly into recursive resolvers. Once a year, a public ceremony involving trusted keyholders from around the globe is held to cryptographically rotate the root keys, maintaining the foundational trust layer of the modern digital globe.

3. The Treaty of Kahn and Cerf: Shouting Across the Windy Canyon

Your phone now holds the destination numbers: 157.240.1.35. It must send the letter. But the raw infrastructure of the internet—the physical Internet Protocol (IP) layer—is fundamentally chaotic and unreliable. IP is a "best-effort," packet-switched network. A large message is chopped into smaller packets, and each packet is thrown into the network. Packets might take different physical routes: one might fly through a satellite over the Pacific, another might zoom through an undersea fiber under the Atlantic.

Packets can arrive out of order, get corrupted by electrical interference, or be silently dropped by overloaded routers along the way. IP does not care; it has no concept of order, memory, or state. To build a reliable conversation on top of this chaos, we rely on TCP (Transmission Control Protocol), designed by Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn in 1974.

Let us recall our analogy of the two Victorian gentlemen standing on opposite sides of a vast, windy canyon, trying to exchange a long manuscript by writing sentences on numbered cards and throwing them across the gap. The wind represents the flaky IP packet-switched physical links. To achieve reliable delivery, the gentlemen must establish a highly structured, mutually agreed-upon protocol. This is the TCP Three-Way Handshake.

Plate III: The TCP Three-Way Handshake

The handshake begins when the client device constructs a small TCP segment with the SYN (Synchronize) flag set. The client must select a random Initial Sequence Number (ISN)—let us say X = 1000. Why random? If the sequence numbers were predictable (e.g., starting at zero every time), an attacker could easily craft a spoofed packet with the next expected sequence number and inject malicious data into your connection. This is a TCP Session Hijacking Attack, and randomizing the ISN is the primary shield against it.

The client throws this card across the canyon. The server catches it. It reads the SYN flag, and replies by throwing its own card: SYN-ACK (Synchronize-Acknowledge). On this card, the server asserts two values: first, it sets the acknowledgment number to X + 1 (1001), indicating "I successfully caught your card 1000, and I expect your next card to be numbered 1001." Second, the server asserts its own random Initial Sequence Number, let us say Y = 5000.

The client catches the SYN-ACK card. It registers the acknowledgment, and throws the final card: ACK (Acknowledge). It sets the sequence number to 1001, and the acknowledgment number to Y + 1 (5001). The server catches this card. Both sides have now formally verified that their communication path is clear, and they have established a shared framework for numbering and keeping track of every single sentence to follow.

But TCP is much more than a handshake; it is an incredibly complex engineering machine designed to handle network congestion dynamically. First, TCP uses the Sliding Window Protocol to manage Flow Control. A fast server could easily flood a slow mobile device by sending data faster than the client’s CPU can process it. To prevent this, every TCP packet carries a Window Size header. This number represents how much free buffer space remains in the receiver's memory. The sender is strictly forbidden from launching more unacknowledged data than the current window size. As the client processes data, its buffer empties, it advertises a larger window, and the sender speeds up.

Second, TCP uses Congestion Control to manage the global network lanes. If every computer sent packets as fast as physically possible, the internet's core routers would immediately choke, their buffers would overflow, and the entire network would experience a catastrophic collapse known as congestion collapse. To prevent this, TCP is designed to be a "good citizen." It uses mathematical algorithms like Tahoe, Reno, or Cubic to dynamically sense how crowded the network is.

To compute when a packet is considered "lost," the TCP stack maintains a running estimation of the Retransmission Timeout (RTO). This is dynamically calculated using Jacobson/Karels algorithm:

\[\text{RTT\_Delta} = \text{Measured\_RTT} - \text{SRTT}\]
\[\text{SRTT} \leftarrow \text{SRTT} + \alpha \cdot \text{RTT\_Delta}\]
\[\text{RTTVAR} \leftarrow \text{RTTVAR} + \beta \cdot (|\text{RTT\_Delta}| - \text{RTTVAR})\]
\[\text{RTO} = \text{SRTT} + 4 \cdot \text{RTTVAR}\]
where \(\text{SRTT}\) is the Smoothed Round Trip Time, \(\text{RTTVAR}\) is the Round Trip Time Variation, and standard values for the filter gains are \(\alpha = 0.125\) and \(\beta = 0.25\). This ensures the timeout window tightly wraps around actual network conditions.

When the connection starts, TCP enters Slow Start. It starts by sending a single packet. If it receives an ACK, it doubles its output to two packets. Then four. Then eight. It increases exponentially until it hits the Slow Start Threshold (ssthresh)or encounters packet loss. Packet loss is registered when an ACK fails to return within the RTO, or when the client receives three duplicate ACKs (indicating that subsequent packets arrived, but a gap remains).

Once loss is detected, TCP immediately drops its transmission window and enters Congestion Avoidance, utilizing an Additive Increase Multiplicative Decrease (AIMD) algorithm. The mathematical formulas governing the congestion window size (\(W\)) are defined as:

\[W(t+1) = W(t) + \frac{1}{W(t)} \quad \text{upon receiving an ACK (Additive Increase)}\]
\[W(t+1) = W(t) \times \beta \quad \text{upon detecting packet loss (Multiplicative Decrease)}\]
where \(\beta\) is typically configured to \(0.5\) in standard Reno implementations, representing a sharp drop of half the sending rate.

Let us trace the evolution of these algorithms:

  • TCP Tahoe (1988): When packet loss occurs, Tahoe cuts the congestion window back to exactly 1 Maximum Segment Size (MSS) and enters Slow Start again, which causes severe throughput degradation.
  • TCP Reno (1990): Introduced Fast Recovery. If three duplicate ACKs are received, Reno cuts the window in half instead of resetting it to 1, avoiding the slow start penalty and dramatically smoothing throughput.
  • TCP Cubic (2008): The default in modern Linux systems. Instead of linear growth, Cubic uses a cubic mathematical function of time since the last loss event to scale the window. This makes it highly aggressive on high-bandwidth, high-latency links (fat pipes) and extremely fair on slower links.

4. TLS Handshake: Whispering Secrets in a Public Square

With our TCP connection established, we have a reliable stream of packets. But this stream is entirely open. If we send our HTTP request now, it is transmitted in plain text. Any router, ISP, or malicious actor sitting on your local coffee shop Wi-Fi can easily sniff your packets, extract your credentials, or tamper with the payload to change the behavior of your application. We must wrap our connection in an unbreakable, cryptographically secure shield. This is the duty of Transport Layer Security (TLS).

The history of web encryption is a dramatic tale of mathematical warfare. It began in 1995 with Netscape’s Secure Sockets Layer (SSL 2.0). The early web was entirely open; passwords and bank details floated as plain text. SSL was designed by legendary cryptographer Taher Elgamal as a way to hide secrets in plain sight using public-key cryptography. Over the next two decades, SSL evolved into TLS, culminating in the streamlined, secure TLS 1.3 in 2018.

Older versions of the protocol (like SSL 2.0, 3.0, and TLS 1.0) were plagued by structural vulnerabilities:

  • POODLE (Padding Oracle On Downgraded Legacy Encryption): Exploited SSL 3.0's handling of padding bytes in block ciphers, allowing attackers to decrypt cookies and session tokens.
  • BEAST (Browser Exploit Against SSL/TLS): Exploited initialization vectors in Cipher Block Chaining (CBC) ciphers to recover plaintext data.
  • CRIME (Compression Ratio Info-leak Made Easy): Exploited HTTP compression headers to leak session IDs.

To eliminate these vulnerabilities, TLS 1.3 (standardized in RFC 8446 in 2018) executed a massive purge. It removed all insecure ciphers (like RC4, Triple-DES, and CBC-mode ciphers), banned compression at the TLS layer, and modernized the handshake. Let us trace the differences between the older TLS 1.2 Handshake and the streamlined TLS 1.3 Handshake.

In TLS 1.2, establishing an encrypted session required two full Round Trip Times (2-RTT) of negotiation. The client sent a ClientHello, the server replied with ServerHello and its certificate, the client verified the cert and negotiated keys, and finally, they exchanged change-cipher-spec cards. This added significant latency—often doubling the time required to establish a connection.

TLS 1.3 slashes this to exactly one Round Trip Time (1-RTT) by making an educated guess. Because the industry has converged on a small set of highly secure cryptographic groups (like Curve25519), the client does not wait for the server's choice. Instead, in its very first ClientHello, it sends its public key share based on Curve25519.

The server receives this, immediately computes the shared secret, packages its own key share, its signed digital certificate, and a cryptographic handshake signature, and fires it back. By the time the server's response lands back at the client, both sides have derived the encryption keys and can immediately begin sending secure application data.

Plate IV: The Cryptographic TLS 1.3 Handshake

Let us look at the mathematics behind the Diffie-Hellman Key Exchange that powers this magic. It is built upon the Discrete Logarithm Problem inside finite fields. The client and server agree on a large prime number p and a base generator g. The client selects a secret private key a (a massive random number) and computes its public key share:

\[A = g^a \pmod p\]
The client sends A to the server.

The server selects its own secret private key b and computes its public share:

\[B = g^b \pmod p\]
The server sends B to the client. Now, the client computes the shared secret:
\[S = B^a \pmod p = (g^b)^a \pmod p = g^{ab} \pmod p\]
The server computes the shared secret:
\[S = A^b \pmod p = (g^a)^b \pmod p = g^{ab} \pmod p\]

Both sides have arrived at the exact same shared key S without ever transmitting a, b, or S over the wire. An eavesdropper sitting in the middle only catches g, p, A, and B. To find S, they must solve for a or b from the equation \(A = g^a \pmod p\).

In modular arithmetic, when p is a massive prime (typically 2048 bits long), there is no known efficient classical algorithm to calculate this discrete logarithm. It would take a supercomputer billions of years of continuous computation to extract the keys, ensuring that your secret remains completely private. In modern systems, this is optimized further using Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECDHE) over curves like Curve25519. The mathematics operates on the algebraic structure of elliptic curves over finite fields, where the group operation replaces modular multiplication, and finding the scalar factor \(d\) from a point \(Q = dP\) constitutes the incredibly difficult Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem (ECDLP).

An elliptic curve over a finite field \(F_p\) is defined by the Weierstrass equation:

\[y^2 = x^3 + ax + b \pmod p\]
Points on the curve, along with a special point at infinity, form an abelian group under point addition. To perform a key exchange, both parties agree on a base point \(G\) on the curve. The client selects a private integer \(k_{client}\) and computes the public point:
\[Q_{client} = k_{client} \cdot G\]
using scalar point multiplication. The server selects its private \(k_{server}\) and computes:
\[Q_{server} = k_{server} \cdot G\]
They exchange their public points \(Q_{client}\) and \(Q_{server}\). The client then computes:
\[S = k_{client} \cdot Q_{server} = k_{client} \cdot (k_{server} \cdot G)\]
while the server computes:
\[S = k_{server} \cdot Q_{client} = k_{server} \cdot (k_{client} \cdot G)\]
Because scalar multiplication is associative, both arrive at the identical coordinate \(S\), which is hashed to generate the symmetric keys.

5. The Under-Ocean Dash: The Physics of the Fiber Transit

Once the cryptographic handshake completes, the HTTP request is parsed, wrapped inside encrypted TLS records, sliced into TCP segments, labeled with IP headers, and converted into raw bits. If you are a user in Mumbai liking a post uploaded by a photographer in New York, those bits must travel roughly 8,000 miles. They fly out of your phone’s antenna as electromagnetic waves at 2.4 GHz or 5 GHz, are captured by your home router, pass through local copper lines, and hit the core internet trunk lines.

To cross the massive oceanic gaps, the signal must leave the air and enter the cold, pitch-black depths of the oceans. It enters the Submarine Fiber Optic Network. These undersea cables are marvels of modern engineering. They are not floating wires; they are heavy, armored cables laid along the mountain ranges and deep trenches of the ocean floors. At the core of these cables are thin strands of highly purified silica glass (fiber). These glass strands are so pure that light can travel through them for miles without significant attenuation.

The physics of fiber transit is governed by Total Internal Reflection and Snell’s Law. The glass fiber is designed with two distinct layers: an inner core with a high refractive index \(n_1\), surrounded by a protective cladding layer with a lower refractive index \(n_2\). When a light pulse is injected into the core at an angle greater than the critical angle \(\theta_c\):

\[\theta_c = \arcsin\left(\frac{n_2}{n_1}\right)\]
the light cannot escape into the cladding. Instead, it is reflected 100% back into the core, bouncing down the glass channel in a continuous zigzag pattern at roughly two-thirds the speed of light in a vacuum (~200,000 kilometers per second).

Snell's Law defines the relationship between the angles of incidence and refraction:

\[n_1 \sin(\theta_1) = n_2 \sin(\theta_2)\]
When light travels from a high refractive index medium (core) to a low index medium (cladding), refraction ceases entirely at the critical angle, resulting in perfect internal reflection.

But even the purest glass introduces physical obstacles:

  • Attenuation: Microscopic impurities in the glass scatter and absorb the light over long distances, causing the signal to fade. This scattering is primarily caused by Rayleigh scattering, where density fluctuations in the glass at the sub-wavelength scale scatter light in all directions.
  • Chromatic Dispersion: A laser pulse is composed of multiple wavelengths of light. Because the index of refraction of glass varies slightly with wavelength, different colors of light travel at slightly different speeds, causing the pulse to spread out and blur over thousands of miles. If left uncorrected, adjacent pulses merge together, corrupting the message.

To combat attenuation, specialized Erbium-Doped Fiber Amplifiers (EDFAs) are spliced into the cable every 50 to 70 kilometers. These amplifiers do not convert the light to electricity and back. Instead, they use a laser to excite erbium ions embedded inside a segment of the fiber. When the weak, incoming optical signal passes through, it triggers stimulated emission, amplifying the light pulses directly in the optical domain.

To power these amplifiers on the ocean floor, the cables carry an inner copper conductor that runs a continuous electrical current of up to 10,000 Volts from high-voltage stations on the coasts. Furthermore, to maximize bandwidth, we use Wavelength Division Multiplexing (WDM). Instead of sending one single light stream down the glass, we shoot dozens of independent lasers down the exact same fiber strand simultaneously, each operating at a slightly different color (wavelength).

At the receiving coast, a prism-like demultiplexer separates the colors back into independent streams. Through this optical orchestra, a single cable no thicker than a garden hose can carry up to 250 Terabits of data per second across the Atlantic.

6. The Digital Customs Gate: Load Balancing and Consistent Hashing

After crossing the ocean floor in a few dozen milliseconds, the pulses of light arrive at a coastal landing station in New Jersey, travel down land-based trunks, and enter Instagram's primary data center. A global application handles hundreds of thousands of requests per second. If all these requests landed on a single physical machine, the network cards would saturate instantly, the CPU would lock up under the thread overhead, and the machine would crash. We must distribute the load.

The request first encounters the Load Balancer. Load balancers operate at two primary levels:

  • Layer 4 (L4) Load Balancing: Operates at the transport layer (TCP/IP). It does not decrypt the packets or look at the HTTP headers. It simply inspects the source IP and port, and forwards the TCP stream to a server using fast packet manipulation (such as NAT or Direct Server Return) in the kernel.
  • Layer 7 (L7) Load Balancing: Operates at the application layer. It decrypts the TLS connection, parses the HTTP headers, evaluates the path (e.g., routing /v1/posts/ to a post microservice and /v1/users/ to a user microservice), and makes smart routing decisions.

Let us look at the mathematics of routing algorithms. A basic algorithm is Round Robin, which simply sends request #1 to Server A, request #2 to Server B, and so on. But in modern distributed systems caching is critical. If Server A has cached your user profile in its local memory and the next request lands on Server B, Server B must execute a slow query to the database, rendering the cache useless.

To solve this, we use Consistent Hashing (pioneered by David Karger et al. in 1997). In a standard hashing scheme, you might map a request to a server using a modulo operation:

\[\text{Server} = \text{hash}(\text{user\_id}) \pmod N\]
where N is the number of active servers in your cluster. While simple, this algorithm has a fatal flaw: if one server crashes (N becomes N - 1) or a new server is added (N becomes N + 1), the result of the modulo operation changes for almost every single key. The entire cache of your system is wiped out in a single instant, triggering a catastrophic stampede of database queries.

Consistent Hashing solves this by mapping both servers and request keys onto a circular ring (the hash ring). The hash ring has a range of \(0\) to \(2^{32} - 1\). Below is a fully realized JavaScript class demonstrating this consistent hashing ring mechanics with virtual nodes:

The useful beginner version is the role being played: which actor asks, which actor verifies, which actor stores memory, and which actor answers.

Under this mathematical design, if a server is removed or added, only the keys that immediately preceded that server on the ring are affected. The vast majority of the ring remains completely undisturbed, and over \(90\%\) of your caches survive the cluster topology change untouched. To ensure an even distribution of keys (avoiding hotspots where a single server has to handle a disproportionate segment of the circle), we introduce virtual nodes. Each physical server is mapped to dozens of independent locations on the ring using separate salt strings, balancing the load with mathematical elegance.

7. The Vault Entrance: Engine Room Validation and Committing to Stone

The load balancer forwards the TCP stream to the chosen application server. The server’s network card receives the packets, and the operating system places them in the TCP receive buffer. A high-performance web server (like NGINX) using an asynchronous, event-driven loop (powered by the epoll system call on Linux) registers that the socket is ready for reading. It reads the bytes, decrypts the TLS wrapper, parses the HTTP text headers, and passes the request payload to the application code.

Let us look at the mechanics of the event loop. In primitive multi-threaded web servers, each incoming TCP connection was allocated a dedicated operating system thread. If a thousand users connected, the server ran a thousand threads. While simple, this design scales poorly because threads are heavy resource structures. Each thread requires up to 8MB of stack memory, and when thousands of threads are active, the CPU wastes massive amounts of time performing context switches—saving CPU state, flushing cache lines, and swapping memory maps.

NGINX solves this using an asynchronous model. A single NGINX worker thread can monitor thousands of concurrent connections. It does this by using the Linux epoll system call. Instead of blocking on a read socket, NGINX registers all active connection sockets with the kernel's epoll registry. NGINX then executes a single loop. It asks the kernel: "Are any of these ten thousand sockets ready to read or write?" The kernel blocks the worker thread until a socket changes state. When a packet arrives, the kernel places it in the epoll ready list and wakes the thread, which processes the socket without ever spawning a new thread or performing a thread context switch.

The application code (such as a Node.js or Rust service) is a highly structured engine room. It executes several sequential checks:

  1. Security & Authentication: The server parses the Authorization: Bearer ... header. It extracts a JSON Web Token (JWT). It validates the cryptographic signature using the public key of your auth server. It checks the expiration claim (exp) to verify that the token hasn't expired. It extracts your unique identity, user_id: "harshit_87".
  2. Input Validation & Sanitization: The server reads the JSON body. It parses the schema, verifying that action is a literal "like" and post_id is a valid positive integer. This is the security shield: if the server skipped this check, an attacker could inject malicious SQL commands (SQL Injection) or overflow buffers.
  3. Business Logic Execution: The server checks if your user account has blocked the creator of the post, or if the post is private. If cleared, it proceeds to execute the update.

To make the update extremely fast, the server first contacts a Redis Cache. It updates an in-memory sorted set tracking the total likes for post 928374. Redis uses highly optimized internal structures—such as skip lists and hash tables—to perform read-write operations in \(O(\log N)\) or \(O(1)\) time, all in RAM. But memory is volatile; if the power cuts out, the cache vanishes. We must commit the record to permanent stone.

The application server opens a database transaction with the PostgreSQL Database. It issues a query: the formal instruction. Inside the database engine, this insert is processed with strict ACID compliance:

  • Atomicity: The like record and the count update must succeed together. If one fails, both are rolled back as if nothing happened.
  • Consistency: The database enforces unique constraints—you cannot like the same post twice. If you try, the database rejects the transaction.
  • Isolation: Other users querying the likes count at the same millisecond will not see your half-committed like until it is fully finished.
  • Durability: Once the database confirms success, the record must survive even if a sudden power blackout strikes the data center.

To guarantee Durability without slowing down performance, the database first writes the transaction details to a Write-Ahead Log (WAL) on non-volatile SSD storage. The WAL is a simple, append-only binary log. Writing sequentially to the WAL is incredibly fast because it bypasses the slow random I/O associated with navigating down large B-Tree index pages. Instead, it appends a sequential representation of the changes directly to a circular binary file. The OS caches the index changes in RAM and flushes them to the data block files in batches. If power is severed, Postgres scans the WAL from the last checkpoint upon reboot, playing forward any committed changes and scrubbing out uncommitted traces, restoring the system to a perfectly consistent state.

Only after the WAL bytes are safely written does the database return a success signal. The actual B-Tree indexes on the disk can be updated lazily in the background; if the server crashes before they are updated, the database reads the WAL during reboot to perfectly reconstruct its state. The transaction is committed. The database returns success.

The application server builds a success response letter: HTTP/1.1 200 OK with a JSON body. It hands the response to the OS TCP stack, which encrypts it via TLS, slices it into packets, and launches it back down the deep-sea optical glass pathways. The packets bounce across the ocean trenches, fly through local cell towers, and land on your phone’s antenna.

Your browser parses the success envelope, the V8 engine triggers a state update, and the heart icon on your screen transforms into a vibrant, warm red.

But here is a final secret of systems design: Your phone did not wait for this response before showing you the red heart. If you had to wait for the entire round-trip journey—DNS, TCP handshake, TLS handshake, load balancing, application routing, database index writes, and the return trip—the heart icon would take two hundred milliseconds to light up. In the hyper-optimized attention economy, this two-hundred-millisecond delay feels sluggish. It feels broken.

To create the illusion of instant telepathy, modern mobile apps use Optimistic UI Updates. The moment your finger taps the screen, the application's local JavaScript/Swift engine changes the heart icon from white to red and increments the counter immediately. It assumes the request will succeed. It updates the local interface in memory, while in the background, the request packet is quietly generated and slung over the internet.

If the request succeeds (as it does 99.9% of the time), the app does nothing; it simply validates the local state. If the request fails (e.g., because your phone lost its connection, or the server rejected the token), the app catches the error, rolls back the local state, turns the heart back to white, decrements the counter, and shows a subtle error banner. It is a grand, beautiful illusion. We write software to project a frictionless reality onto a universe that is full of physical friction, packet drops, and distance.
