Chapter the Ninth

Authentication & Authorization

"On the cryptographic distinction between proving identity and asserting privilege, the structural trade-offs of session stores and token chains, and what the tragedy of Shakuntala’s lost signet ring teaches us about stateless revocation."
♦ ✦ ♦

In the monumental classical dramatic tradition of ancient India, there is a legendary story that has survived across two millennia—the tragedy of Shakuntala and King Dushyanta, immortalized by the poet Kalidasa in the fourth century CE.¹ The King, visiting a serene forest hermitage, falls deeply in love with the maiden Shakuntala and marries her in secret. When the time comes for him to return to his palace in the capital, he is faced with a logical synchronization challenge. How will the palace guards, the high priests, and the royal administration verify her identity when she eventually arrives at the city walls?

Dushyanta does not run a stateful session registry. He does not send a royal courier back to Pataliputra to log her name in the physical marriage ledgers in the palace archives. Instead, he opts for a **Stateless Token-Based Authentication System**. He takes his personal, gold-chased signet ring (*Anguliyaka*), carved with the unique royal crest, and slides it onto Shakuntala’s finger. This ring is a cryptographic masterpiece. It is a stateless, tamper-proof, and universally recognizable token of authenticity. Any guard in the kingdom can inspect the physical carving on the ring and verify the signature without needing to send a runner back to the central palace database.

But then comes the tragedy. On her journey to the capital, while crossing the rushing waters of the Shachi tirtha river, Shakuntala bends down to pray. The signet ring slips from her finger, falls into the dark currents, and is immediately swallowed by a large fish.

When Shakuntala arrives at the royal court, she stands before Dushyanta. She presents her claims. But because a powerful curse has wiped the King's memory, he has no recollection of her. He searches his private, internal mental database—his active session cache—and finds nothing. He demands proof. He asks for the signet ring.

Shakuntala reaches for her hand, but her finger is bare. The stateless token is gone. Because the King has no stateful registry in the palace, and because the stateless token has been lost, Dushyanta has no way to verify her identity. He rejects her claims, casts her out of the court, and she collapses in tears outside the palace gates.

The ring is eventually recovered. A fisherman catches the fish, cuts it open, finds the signet ring, and presents it to the King. The moment Dushyanta holds the ring, the physical token restores his memory, and he is struck by overwhelming grief at his own administrative failure.

This classical drama is the ultimate mythological metaphor for the structural design trade-offs of **Stateful Sessions** versus **Stateless Tokens (JWTs)**, and the absolute hazard of **Token Revocation Failure**.

When we design a web server, we are constantly faced with Dushyanta's dilemma:

Should we maintain an active, stateful registry of every logged-in user in our central database, checking the ledger on every single incoming request? Or should we hand the user a stateless, cryptographic token—a digital signet ring—that they carry in their browser, allowing our servers to verify their identity without querying the database?

If we choose the stateless token, we buy massive, horizontal scalability. But if a user’s token is stolen, or if we need to log them out immediately because their account was compromised, how do we revoke a digital ring that is swimming in the wild, when our servers have no central registry to cancel it?

I. Authentication vs. Authorization: The Boundary Line

Before we look at the engineering implementations, we must establish a strict semantic distinction between the two load-bearing pillars of server security: **Authentication** and **Authorization**.

Many developers treat these terms as interchangeable synonyms. This is a severe conceptual error that leads to fragile, insecure codebase designs.

Dimension Authentication (AuthN) Authorization (AuthZ)
Core Question "Who are you? Are you truly who you claim to be?" "What are you allowed to do? Do you have permission?"
Verification Method Passwords, Passkeys, TOTP (MFA), JWT signatures. Roles (RBAC), attributes (ABAC), ACL matrices.
Execution Phase Pre-execution boundary (Edge Middleware). Mid-execution (Controller/Service domain check).
Standard Failure Response 401 Unauthorized (Authentication failed / missing). 403 Forbidden (Authenticated, but permission denied).

Authentication (AuthN) is the act of verifying a claims identity. When a user logs in by submitting a username and a password, or by presenting a cryptographic signature from a physical YubiKey passkey, they are proving that they are the unique owner of that account. If the check succeeds, we establish their identity and attach a unique identifier (like user ID 42) to the request context.

Authorization (AuthZ) is the act of evaluating privilege. Once we know the user is ID 42, we must decide if they are allowed to execute the specific action they have requested.

If user 42 attempts to delete an invoice, the authorization engine evaluates their permissions: Is user 42 an administrator? Do they own this specific invoice? Has their account been flagged as suspended by the billing department? Even if their authentication is 100% valid (proven by their signet ring), the authorization check will return a 403 Forbidden response if they do not have the privilege to modify that resource.

II. Stateful Sessions: The Traditional Registry

The classic model of web security—which dominated the internet from the invention of HTTP cookies in 1994 until the mid-2010s—is Stateful Session Management.

In this model, the server behaves like a strict Mauryan clerk who logs every marriage and transaction in a massive leather book. When a user submits their password, the server verifies it, generates a long, cryptographically random string of bytes called a Session ID (usually 32 bytes of entropy generated from a cryptographically secure pseudo-random number generator, or CSPRNG), and writes this ID as a new row in an active database table: 

CREATE TABLE sessions (
  id VARCHAR(64) PRIMARY KEY,
  user_id INT REFERENCES users(id),
  expires_at TIMESTAMP NOT NULL,
  created_at TIMESTAMP DEFAULT NOW()
);

The server then sends this Session ID back to the user’s browser inside a special HTTP header: 

Set-Cookie: session_id=z8a9f2bc7d83ef0a1; Path=/; Secure; HttpOnly; SameSite=Lax

On every subsequent request, the browser automatically attaches this cookie. When the server receives the request, it extracts the Session ID from the cookie, runs a SQL query (or a fast cache lookup in Redis) to locate the row in the sessions table, checks if the expiration date has passed, and retrieves the corresponding user_id.

Cookie Security Parameters

Because cookies are stored automatically by the browser and transmitted on every request, they are the primary target for malicious scripts. To shield this cookie boundary, you must configure three critical security parameters:

  • HttpOnly: This is a vital security flag. By setting HttpOnly, you instruct the browser that the cookie must never be accessed by client-side JavaScript. If an attacker successfully executes a Cross-Site Scripting (XSS) attack on your site, they can run scripts to steal local storage data, but they cannot read your session_id cookie. It remains completely invisible to the DOM, secure inside the browser’s private network sandbox.
  • Secure: This flag instructs the browser that the cookie must only be transmitted over encrypted **HTTPS** channels. If a user connects to a public airport Wi-Fi network, this flag prevents the browser from sending the Session ID in cleartext over unencrypted HTTP, shielding it from packet-sniffing and man-in-the-middle attacks.
  • SameSite (Lax/Strict): This is the primary defense against Cross-Site Request Forgery (CSRF) attacks. Suppose an attacker builds a malicious site that contains an invisible image pointing to your bank’s API: <img src="https://yourbank.com/api/transfer?to=attacker&amount=1000">. If a user visits the attacker's site while logged into their bank, a naive browser will automatically attach the user's session cookie to that image request, executing the money transfer without their consent. By setting SameSite=Lax (the modern default) or SameSite=Strict, you instruct the browser that the cookie must never be sent along with cross-site requests initiated by third-party sites, completely neutralizing the CSRF threat vector.

The Scaling Wall of Stateful Sessions

While stateful sessions are incredibly secure—allowing the server to revoke any session instantly by deleting the row from the database—they hit a massive architectural wall when you scale horizontally behind a load balancer.

Suppose you run three servers: Server A, Server B, and Server C. A user logs in, and their session row is written to Server A’s local, in-memory cache.

A second later, the user requests their profile page. The load balancer routes the request to Server B. Server B checks its local cache, finds no record of session z8a9f2bc7d83ef0a1, and redirects the user to the login page.

To prevent this, you must migrate from local in-memory caches to a centralized, shared session cache—typically a clustered, high-performance Redis database. While this solves the synchronization problem, it introduces a single point of failure and a massive latency bottleneck: on every single network request, your application servers must execute an external network call to Redis to check if the session is valid, consuming vast amounts of network bandwidth and adding milliseconds of latency to every interaction.

III. Stateless Tokens: JSON Web Tokens (JWT)

To bypass the database bottleneck of session stores, modern distributed architectures use JSON Web Tokens (JWT), formalized in RFC 7519.²

A JWT is a digital, cryptographic signet ring. It is completely self-contained and stateless. It carries all user details in its own body and signs them cryptographically. When the server receives a JWT, it does not query any database. It simply runs a high-speed cryptographic signature check on the token. If the signature matches, it trusts the payload implicitly.

A JWT is represented as a single, flat string of text divided into three distinct parts by dots (.): 

eyJhbGciOiJIUzI1NiIsInR5cCI6IkpXVCJ9
.
eyJzdWIiOiI0MiIsIm5hbWUiOiJIYXJzaGl0IiwiYWRtaW4iOnRydWUsImV4cCI6MTgwMTIzNDU2N30
.
SflKxwRJSMeKKF2QT4fwpMeJf36POk6yJV_adQssw5c

Let us decode these three components:

1. The Header

The first section is a Base64URL-encoded JSON object that specifies the cryptographic algorithms used to sign the token: 

{
  "alg": "HS256",
  "typ": "JWT"
}

2. The Payload

The second section is the actual core data—the **Claims**. This is a Base64URL-encoded JSON object tracking user details, permissions, and metadata: 

{
  "sub": "42",
  "name": "Harshit",
  "admin": true,
  "exp": 1801234567
}

Notice the key metadata claim: exp (Expiration). Because the token is stateless, the server needs to know when the signet ring expires. The server reads this timestamp during validation. If the current time is greater than the exp timestamp, the token is rejected as expired.

3. The Signature

The load-bearing pillar that prevents the client from tampering with the payload. The signature is calculated by taking the Base64URL-encoded header, appending a dot, appending the Base64URL-encoded payload, and signing the combined string using a secret cryptographic key: 

Signature = HMACSHA256(
  base64UrlEncode(header) + "." + base64UrlEncode(payload),
  secret_key
)

If an attacker attempts to modify the payload to make themselves an administrator (changing "admin": false to "admin": true), the header-payload hash changes. Because the attacker does not know the server's private secret_key, they cannot calculate a matching signature. The server receives the tampered token, runs the signature check, realizes they do not align, and rejects the request as a malicious forgery.

Symmetric vs. Asymmetric Signing

How you sign your tokens has profound security implications for your service boundaries:

  • Symmetric Signatures (HS256): Uses the same secret key to both sign and verify the token. This is extremely fast, but it introduces a major key distribution risk. Every microservice in your network must know the shared secret key to verify incoming tokens. If a single junior developer leaks the secret from service twelve, or if a single microservice container is compromised, the attacker can forge valid tokens for the entire ecosystem.
  • Asymmetric Signatures (RS256/ES256): Uses a public/private key pair. The central Authentication Server signs the token using a secret Private Key. All downstream microservices only need to know the corresponding Public Key to verify the signature. Downstream microservices can verify tokens securely, but even if they are fully compromised, the attacker cannot forge new tokens because they do not have the private key, which remains locked behind the authentication server’s high-security boundary.

The Heretical alg: none Vulnerability

A few years ago, security researchers discovered a massive, systemic flaw in many popular JWT validation libraries: **The `alg: none` exploit**.

The JWT specification includes an algorithm option called none, designed for testing environments where signatures are not required. An attacker can craft a token, change the header to {"alg": "none"}, modify the payload to set their user role to admin, strip the signature entirely (leaving the trailing dot), and submit it to the server.

A naive validation library, reading the header, would switch its validation engine to none, skip the signature verification, and accept the modified payload as authentic! Modern libraries have patched this catastrophic gap, but it stands as a reminder of the extreme risks of letting client-controlled headers dictate server security protocols.

IV. The Revocation Paradox: Access vs. Refresh Tokens

The most significant, load-bearing challenge of stateless tokens is The Revocation Paradox.

Suppose a user’s laptop is stolen, or their account is hacked. They log in from a backup device and click "Log out of all devices."

If you are using stateful sessions, this is trivial: you delete the rows in the sessions database table. The next time the stolen laptop connects, the Session ID is missing from the database, and access is denied instantly.

But if you are using stateless JWTs, the stolen laptop presents a token that was signed five minutes ago with a 30-day expiration date. The server does not check any database. It runs the signature check, verifies that the cryptographic signature is mathematically valid, and grants access. The hacker can continue accessing the account for thirty days, and the user has no way to cancel the physical token because it is swimming in the wild!

To solve this paradox, we use a two-token design: Short-lived Access Tokens + Long-lived Refresh Tokens.

Plate XIX: Symmetrical Access vs. Refresh Token Flow

Let us trace this multi-token design:

  1. The Access Token (Short-lived JWT): The user carries a short-lived access JWT, typically with an expiration date of **fifteen minutes**. This token is stateless, containing user details, permissions, and roles. The server verifies it cryptographically on every request, completely bypassing the database.
  2. The Refresh Token (Long-lived, Stateful key): The user also receives a long-lived Refresh Token, typically with an expiration date of **thirty days**. Unlike the access token, the Refresh Token is not a JWT. It is a stateful, random string of bytes, stored in the database.
  3. The Token Exchange: When the client’s fifteen-minute access token expires, the client makes a background request to the token endpoint, presenting their Refresh Token. The server queries the database to check if the Refresh Token is valid. If it is, the server issues a brand-new fifteen-minute access JWT.

This architecture offers the best of both worlds. For fifteen minutes at a time, your servers run with absolute, database-free, horizontal scalability.

If a user needs to revoke access immediately—either because their laptop was stolen or their account compromised—they delete the long-lived Refresh Token from the database. The stolen laptop will continue to have access for a maximum of fifteen minutes (until the current access JWT expires). When it attempts to exchange the expired access token for a new one, the server will check the database, realize the Refresh Token is missing or revoked, and block further access, bringing the security window back to a highly manageable limit.

V. Federated Identity: OAuth 2.0 & OpenID Connect (OIDC)

As the web expanded, a new architectural problem emerged: how do you allow third-party applications to access a user’s data without forcing them to share their raw password?

If you wanted a printing service to print photos from your Google Drive, the legacy web forced you to enter your Google password directly into the printing site's forms. This was a security catastrophe. The printing site could read all your emails, forge your documents, and lock you out of your own account.

To solve this, the industry formalized the OAuth 2.0 framework in RFC 6749, introducing the concept of **Federated Delegation**.

The Authorization Code Flow with PKCE

The gold standard for securing web applications is the Authorization Code Flow, now augmented with PKCE (Proof Key for Code Exchange) to block credential interception attacks:

  1. The Authorization Request: The client app generates a random, cryptographically secure string called the Code Verifier. It runs a SHA-256 hash on this verifier to generate a Code Challenge. The client redirects the user to the central Authorization Server, sending the Code Challenge: 
    GET /authorize?client_id=123&code_challenge=SHA256(verifier)&code_challenge_method=S256
  2. The User Login: The user logs into the Authorization Server securely. They review the requested permissions (scopes) and grant access.
  3. The Code Redirect: The Authorization Server redirects the user’s browser back to the client application, bringing along a temporary, short-lived Authorization Code in the URL query string.
  4. The Secure Token Exchange: The client application sends a direct server-to-server POST request to the token endpoint. It transmits the temporary Authorization Code, and crucially, the raw **Code Verifier** it generated in step one: 
    POST /token
Body: code=temp_code&code_verifier=raw_verifier_string
  5. The Validation Check: The Authorization Server runs the SHA-256 hash on the submitted code_verifier and verifies that it matches the code_challenge sent in step one. By doing this, the server proves that the client requesting the tokens is the exact same client that initiated the login in step one, neutralizing man-in-the-middle interception attacks.
  6. The Token Response: If the check succeeds, the server returns the Access Token and the ID Token to the client.

OpenID Connect (OIDC)

While OAuth 2.0 was designed for **delegated authorization** (granting permission to read files), it did not provide a standardized way to prove **identity**. It was a key, but it did not have your name printed on it.

To solve this, the industry built **OpenID Connect (OIDC)** as a federated identity layer on top of the OAuth 2.0 framework.

OIDC introduces a standardized JWT called the ID Token. The ID token contains standard identity claims (like sub for user ID, iss for issuer, and email). It also mandates a standardized /userinfo endpoint, allowing applications to fetch profile pictures, display names, and language preferences in a clean, language-agnostic format, forming the backbone of the "Sign in with Google/Apple" buttons that dominate the modern web.

VI. Symmetrical Architectural Comparison Matrix

Let us synthesize the physical properties of our three primary security patterns:

Dimension Stateful Sessions Stateless JWTs Federated OIDC
Identity Registry Central database (Redis/SQL). Checked on every single request. None. Verified cryptographically using secret keys. Central Identity Provider (IdP) server cluster.
Revocation Speed Instant. Deleting database row terminates access immediately. Delayed (until access token TTL expires) or via blocklist lookup. Delegated. Controlled by the Identity Provider's token policies.
Horizontal Scalability Poor. Requires synchronized shared memory cache. Excellent. Infinite horizontal scaling without database hits. Excellent. Downstream services delegate parsing to cached keys.
Primary Vulnerability CSRF (Session Hijack via third-party cookies). Token Leakage via XSS, Cryptographic algorithm none exploit. Code Interception, PKCE configuration bypass.

What is the final lesson here?

Authentication is not a simple, passive detail. It is the core boundary that keeps your system secure.

If you rely entirely on a stateless token without managing expiration windows and revocation blocklists, your system behaves like King Dushyanta’s court. A lost signet ring leads to absolute administrative failure.

By understanding the cryptographic internals of JWT signatures, securing cookies with strict HttpOnly flags, and deploying multi-token access/refresh architectures, you ensure that your server remains a resilient bastion. Secure your keys. Validate your signatures. and protect the sanctuary of your data in absolute peace.
