Chapter the Ninth

Authentication & Authorization

"On the cryptographic distinction between proving identity and asserting privilege, the structural trade-offs of session stores and token chains, and what the tragedy of Shakuntala's lost signet ring teaches the architect about stateless revocation."
♦ ✦ ♦

In the classical Sanskrit tradition, Kalidasa's Abhijnanashakuntalam tells the story of King Dushyanta and Shakuntala.¹ The King marries Shakuntala in secret at a forest hermitage, then returns to his palace. When she eventually arrives at the city gates, the guards and administrators must verify her identity.

Dushyanta's solution is elegant: he gives her his personal signet ring, carved with the royal crest. Any guard in the kingdom can inspect the ring and verify its authenticity without sending a messenger back to the palace. This is, in essence, a Stateless Token-Based Authentication System — the ring carries its own proof.

"The signet ring is the ancient prototype of the stateless JSON Web Token. The gold band carries the credential, and the engraving represents the cryptographic signature, verified locally without querying a central registry."

But tragedy strikes. While crossing a river, the ring slips from Shakuntala's finger and is swallowed by a fish. When she reaches the court, Dushyanta — his memory wiped by a curse — finds no record of her in his mind. He demands proof. Her finger is bare. With no central registry and no token, she is turned away.

The ring is eventually recovered by a fisherman and returned to the King. Upon holding it, Dushyanta's memory is restored, and he is overcome with grief at his own administrative failure.

This drama is the ultimate metaphor for the trade-offs between Stateful Sessions and Stateless Tokens, and the danger of Token Revocation Failure. Should a system maintain a central registry of every logged-in user, checked on every request? Or should it hand each user a cryptographic token they carry themselves, letting servers verify identity without any database lookup?

The stateless approach buys massive scalability. But if a token is stolen, how do you revoke a credential that exists only in the wild, with no central registry to cancel it?

I. Authentication vs. Authorization: The Boundary Line

ASIDE Think of Authentication as the outer gatekeeper verifying the seal on a passport, while Authorization is the chamberlain checking whether the traveler's clearance matches the specific vault they are trying to enter.

Before examining the engineering, a strict distinction must be established between the two pillars of server security: Authentication and Authorization. Many developers treat these as interchangeable synonyms — a conceptual error that leads to fragile, insecure designs.

Dimension Authentication (AuthN) Authorization (AuthZ)
Core Question "Who are you? Are you truly who you claim to be?" "What are you allowed to do?"
Verification Method Passwords, passkeys, TOTP (MFA), JWT signatures, TLS client certificates. Roles (RBAC), attributes (ABAC), ACLs, capability tokens.
Execution Phase Edge — API Gateway or middleware, before the request reaches application logic. Mid-execution — inside the controller or service layer.
Failure Response 401 Unauthorized — credentials missing or invalid. 403 Forbidden — authenticated, but lacking permission.

Authentication (AuthN) is the act of verifying a claimed identity. When a user logs in with a password or a cryptographic passkey, they are proving they own that account. If the check succeeds, the system attaches a unique user ID to the request context.

Authorization (AuthZ) is the act of evaluating privilege. Once the system knows the user is, say, user 42, it must decide whether user 42 is allowed to perform the requested action. Even a perfectly authenticated user can be denied — for example, a regular user trying to delete another user's invoice will receive a 403 Forbidden.

Authorization generally follows three structural paradigms:

1. Role-Based Access Control (RBAC)

Users are assigned to Roles (e.g., Editor, Administrator), and each role carries a set of Permissions (e.g., invoice:delete). A user inherits every permission attached to their role. This is simple to implement and fast to query.

In complex environments, roles can be hierarchical. An Admin role automatically inherits all permissions of Editor, which inherits all permissions of Viewer.

RBAC's weakness is Role Explosion. When permissions must vary by department, region, and resource ownership, administrators are forced to create a distinct role for every combination — Editor_DepartmentA_US, Editor_DepartmentB_UK, and so on. The number of roles grows uncontrollably, making the system unmaintainable and prone to misconfiguration.

2. Attribute-Based Access Control (ABAC)

To escape Role Explosion, ABAC evaluates privileges dynamically by inspecting attributes across four dimensions at the moment the request is made:

  • Subject Attributes: The user's department, job title, account status, clearance level.
  • Resource Attributes: The object's owner, creation date, classification level, file type.
  • Action Attributes: The operation being attempted — read, write, delete, export.
  • Environment Attributes: The client's IP range, current time, geographic region, network security level.

For example: "A user can edit an invoice only if they belong to Finance, the invoice status is draft, the request comes from within the corporate network, and it is during business hours (09:00–17:00 UTC)."

CAUTION Because ABAC evaluates multiple dynamic attributes at runtime, it is more computationally expensive than RBAC. Subsecond API latency requirements demand caching attribute sets in fast in-memory stores and optimizing database query paths.

3. The Hampi Gate Check: An Indian Systems Analogy

These abstract concepts have a striking real-world parallel in the Vijayanagara Empire (fourteenth–seventeenth centuries CE). The fortified capital, Hampi, was protected by seven concentric walls with heavily guarded gateways. Access was controlled using stamped pass tokens called Mudras.

Permanent stamped metal Mudras were issued to military commanders and officials. When a guard verified the cast symbol, the gate opened — a simple role-based check. The token mapped directly to a known permission set.

For merchants and foreign traders, a static role check was insufficient. Gatekeepers performed a dynamic, multi-attribute check using ephemeral clay or palm-leaf tokens:

  • Subject: The merchant's guild membership, country of origin, tax exemption status.
  • Resource: The cargo type — war horses required royal permits, local grain did not.
  • Action: Selling in the municipal market (requiring a trade license) vs. merely passing through.
  • Environment: Which gate was used (some goods were restricted to designated entrances), time of day (no merchant entry after the evening temple drums), and seasonal river levels that could close low-lying gates.

The frontier chief stamped the clay with their unique signet and painted a daily-changing dye mark on the side — an ephemeral signature preventing reuse of old tokens. The local gatekeeper validated everything from the token itself, without sending a runner back to the frontier post, completely bypassing the latency of animal-based communication.

II. Password Hashing: The Cryptographic Vault

Before establishing sessions or issuing tokens, the backend must securely verify credentials. Passwords must never be stored in cleartext, nor protected with reversible encryption. They must be stored as one-way, cryptographically secure hashes.

The key distinction: encryption is reversible (data can be decrypted with the right key), while hashing is a one-way transformation. Given a hash, it is computationally impossible to reconstruct the original password.

1. The Fast-Hash Vulnerability

A common beginner mistake is to hash passwords with MD5, SHA-1, or SHA-256. These algorithms were designed for file integrity checks and digital signatures — they are engineered to be extremely fast, running in microseconds.

This speed is catastrophic for password security. A modern GPU cluster can calculate billions of SHA-256 hashes per second. If an attacker steals the database, they can brute-force through trillions of password combinations at negligible cost. Password hashing algorithms must therefore be intentionally slow and resource-heavy, with adjustable computational cost.

2. Bcrypt

Invented by Niels Provos and David Mazières in 1999, Bcrypt is based on the Blowfish cipher. Its core mechanism is a Work Factor (or cost) parameter. Every increment of this value doubles the execution time, letting administrators increase hashing cost as hardware improves.

Bcrypt automatically generates a cryptographically secure 128-bit salt and prepends it to the final hash. This ensures identical passwords produce completely different hashes, defeating precomputed Rainbow Table attacks. The output is a standardized string containing the algorithm version, cost, salt, and hash.

A critical limitation: Bcrypt truncates input passwords at 72 bytes. Characters beyond that point are silently ignored.

SECURITY To support long passphrases without silent truncation, pre-hash the password with a fast hash like SHA-256 before passing it to Bcrypt. This maps any input length to a uniform 32-byte value.

3. PBKDF2

PBKDF2 (Password-Based Key Derivation Function 2), formalized in RFC 2898, applies a pseudorandom function (typically HMAC-SHA256) to the password and salt over a high number of iterations. Each iteration feeds the previous output back into the function, making brute-force increasingly expensive.

PBKDF2's major weakness: it requires negligible memory to compute. This makes it highly susceptible to parallelized GPU and ASIC cracking, since thousands of cores can run simultaneously without hitting any memory bottleneck.

4. Argon2id: The Memory-Hard Standard

Winner of the Password Hashing Competition in 2015 and formalized in RFC 9106, Argon2 is the gold standard for modern password storage. It is explicitly designed to be memory-hard, forcing the hashing process to consume large, parameterizable blocks of RAM. This saturates hardware memory buses and renders GPU/ASIC cracking highly inefficient.

Argon2 comes in three variants:

  • Argon2d: Modifies memory access patterns based on the password itself. Maximum GPU resistance, but vulnerable to cache timing side-channel attacks.
  • Argon2i: Uses password-independent memory access patterns. Immune to cache timing attacks, but slower.
  • Argon2id (recommended): A hybrid — data-independent access on the first pass, data-dependent on subsequent passes. Best of both worlds.

Argon2id's work is controlled by three independent parameters:

Parameter Description Production Range What It Defends Against
Memory How much RAM the algorithm consumes per hash. 64 MB – 256 MB. Fills GPU cache, forcing slow off-chip RAM lookups.
Time (Passes) Number of sequential passes over the memory. 1 – 3 passes. Increases computation duration, raising GPU cost.
Parallelism Number of parallel threads. 2 – 8 threads. Leverages multi-core server hardware efficiently.

5. Hashing as a Denial-of-Service Vector

Heavy password hashing introduces a vulnerability: Authentication Endpoint Exhaustion.

Suppose the login endpoint uses Argon2id configured with 64 MB of memory per check. If a botnet floods the endpoint with 100 login requests per second using fake credentials, the server must run the full Argon2id computation on each one — consuming 6.4 GB of RAM per second — before discovering the credentials are invalid. This triggers the operating system's Out-Of-Memory killer, crashing the service entirely.

Defensive layers include:

  • IP Rate Limiting at the reverse proxy — cap POST requests to the login route per unique IP.
  • Username Throttling — lock out parallel attempts targeting the same account name.
  • Worker Pool Isolation — dedicate a separate thread pool for password validation so authentication floods cannot starve the main API gateway.

III. Stateful Sessions: The Traditional Registry

Once the password is verified, the backend must establish a persistent session with the client. The classic approach is Stateful Session Management.

The server acts like a meticulous scribe recording every transaction in a centralized ledger. When valid credentials arrive, the server generates a cryptographically secure random string — the Session ID (typically 32 bytes of randomness) — and writes it as a key in a centralized store like Redis, mapped to the user's identity, roles, and metadata. This entry also carries a TTL (time-to-live), after which it expires automatically.

1. Signed Cookie Mechanics

To prevent session spoofing, the Session ID is transmitted inside a signed HTTP cookie. The server appends a cryptographic signature (an HMAC) calculated from the Session ID and a secret key known only to the server. When a request arrives, the server recalculates the signature. If it does not match the one attached to the cookie, the request is discarded as a forgery.

SECURITY Cookie signing protects the session store from exhaustion attacks. Without signing, an attacker could flood the server with random forged Session IDs, forcing millions of pointless database lookups. Signing lets the server reject forgeries at the network boundary.

2. Clustered Shared Session Memory

Stateful sessions are highly secure — the server can instantly terminate a session by deleting its key from Redis. But they hit an architectural wall when scaled horizontally across dozens or hundreds of application servers behind a load balancer.

Every request now requires a network round-trip to the centralized Redis cluster. In a modern VPC, this adds 0.5 ms to 1.5 ms per request. At 20,000 requests per second, this cumulative latency consumes significant thread capacity and can exhaust the server's TCP socket pool.

Memory sizing is also critical. Five million concurrent sessions at 1 KB each requires 5 GB of persistent RAM in the Redis cluster. The cluster must be configured with a strict eviction policy (such as Least Recently Used with active TTLs) to prevent memory saturation from crashing the nodes.

3. Browser Security Boundaries

Because cookies are stored automatically by the browser and transmitted on every outgoing request, they are a primary attack target. Three security parameters must be configured on the Set-Cookie header:

  • HttpOnly: Prevents client-side JavaScript from accessing the cookie. If an attacker executes an XSS exploit, they can steal local storage tokens, but the signed session cookie remains invisible to their scripts.
  • Secure: Restricts cookie transmission to encrypted HTTPS channels only, preventing cleartext leakage over unencrypted Wi-Fi.
  • SameSite: The primary defense against Cross-Site Request Forgery (CSRF). Controls whether the browser appends cookies to cross-origin requests:
    • Strict — never sends the cookie on cross-origin requests. Most secure, but degrades user experience (a logged-in user clicking a link from a search engine arrives unauthenticated).
    • Lax — blocks cookies on cross-origin POST forms and AJAX requests, but allows them on safe top-level navigations (clicking a regular hyperlink). This is the standard production default.

IV. Stateless Tokens: JWT vs. PASETO

To eliminate the Redis lookup and database scaling limits of stateful sessions, modern distributed architectures use JSON Web Tokens (JWT), formalized in RFC 7519.

Instead of maintaining a central record of logged-in users, the server serializes the user's identity claims, signs them cryptographically, and hands the token to the client. The client carries their own state on every request in the Authorization: Bearer header. The server verifies the signature mathematically — no database reads required.

1. Common JWT Exploits

A. The "alg: none" Signature Bypass

The JWT spec includes a none algorithm, intended for debugging in isolated environments. A naive validation library reads the alg claim from the token header and dynamically switches its verification strategy. An attacker can exploit this by changing the payload (e.g., setting isAdmin: true), setting the header to {"alg": "none"}, and stripping the signature entirely. The server accepts the forged payload as authentic.

B. Symmetric-to-Asymmetric Key Confusion

A more sophisticated attack targets systems using asymmetric RS256 (public/private key pairs). The attacker downloads the server's public key (intentionally exposed via a JWKS endpoint), then signs a forged token using symmetric HMAC-SHA256, using the public key string as the symmetric secret. If the library does not strictly enforce the allowed algorithm, it reads alg: HS256 from the forged token and verifies the HMAC against the public key string — which matches, since both the attacker and the library used the same key. The server grants access.

Mitigation: Hardcode the allowed algorithm in your verification configuration. Never trust the algorithm declared in the client's token header.

2. PASETO: Platform-Agnostic Security Tokens

PASETO was designed to eliminate JWT's systematic flaws. There is no algorithm negotiation — the developer chooses a strict, non-negotiable Protocol Version.

A PASETO token is a period-separated string: version.purpose.payload.footer.

  • Version: The protocol standard (e.g., v4).
  • Purpose: Either local (symmetric encryption) or public (asymmetric signing).
  • Payload: The encrypted or signed claims.
  • Footer: Optional unencrypted metadata (e.g., key IDs).
Protocol Purpose Cryptographic Suite Guarantees
v4.local Symmetric Encryption XChaCha20-Poly1305 (256-bit AEAD) Confidentiality, Integrity, Replay Defense.
v4.public Asymmetric Signing Ed25519 (Edwards-curve Digital Signatures) Authenticity, Non-Repudiation.

PASETO also prevents Canonicalization Attacks (where boundary markers between joined strings are blurred to forge signatures) by encoding the exact byte length of every input before concatenation — guaranteeing that parameters can never overlap or be parsed ambiguously.

V. Cryptographic Rigor: How Signatures Work

To build robust token verification, the underlying mechanics of symmetric and asymmetric signing must be understood.

1. Symmetric Signing: HMAC-SHA256

HMAC (Hash-based Message Authentication Code) is a symmetric signing algorithm — both the issuer and verifier share a single secret key. It works by hashing the message twice with the key mixed in using two different padding values (an inner padding and an outer padding). This nested structure prevents attackers from appending unauthorized data to a signed message, ensuring data integrity even if the underlying hash function has certain weaknesses.

The key limitation: every server that needs to verify tokens must possess the same secret key. If the key leaks from any single server, the entire system is compromised.

2. Asymmetric Signing: RSA-SHA256 (RS256)

Asymmetric algorithms solve the key distribution problem. The authentication server holds a private key to sign tokens, while downstream services hold the matching public key to verify them. RSA's security rests on the mathematical difficulty of factoring the product of two very large prime numbers — a problem that is practically impossible to solve for key sizes of 2048 bits or larger.

Signing uses the private key to transform a hash of the token into a signature. Verification uses the public key to reverse the transformation and check whether the result matches the token's hash. If they match, the token is authentic.

3. Elliptic Curve Cryptography: ES256 & Ed25519

RSA signatures are large (256–512 bytes) and computationally expensive. Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC) achieves the same security level with dramatically smaller keys and faster operations. ECDSA (ES256) uses the NIST P-256 curve and produces 64-byte signatures — but it has a critical implementation risk: the signing process requires a random number for every signature. If the randomness ever repeats, the private key can be mathematically recovered.

Ed25519 (RFC 8032) addresses this with deterministic signatures — the random number is derived from the private key and message via a hash, eliminating the risk of entropy failures leaking the private key.

4. JSON Web Key Sets (JWKS) and Key Rotation

Authentication servers publish their active public keys at a standardized endpoint: /.well-known/jwks.json. The response contains an array of public keys, each tagged with a unique Key ID (kid).

When a downstream service receives a token, it reads the kid from the header, looks up the matching public key in its local cache, and verifies the signature. If the token carries an unknown kid, the service fetches the JWKS endpoint once to retrieve newly rotated keys.

SECURITY To prevent JWKS Cache Exhaustion attacks — where an attacker floods the server with forged tokens carrying random kid values, forcing millions of outbound HTTP requests — downstream services must rate-limit JWKS fetches and cache results for at least 24 hours.

VI. The Revocation Paradox: Access vs. Refresh Tokens

The primary hazard of stateless authentication: the server cannot revoke a token before it expires, because the server does not track issued tokens.

The solution is a Two-Token Hybrid Protocol: short-lived Access Tokens and long-lived, stateful Refresh Tokens.

Plate XXI: Symmetrical Access vs. Refresh Token Flow

The hybrid architecture operates as follows:

  1. Access Token: A stateless, short-lived JWT (typically 15 minutes). Stored in client memory or a session cookie. Sent on all API requests. Servers verify the signature locally and instantly.
  2. Refresh Token: A long-lived, stateful token (typically 7 days). Stored as an HttpOnly, Lax cookie. Its ID is recorded in the centralized Redis store.
  3. Silent Refresh: When the access token expires, the browser intercepts the failed request and sends a silent POST to /api/refresh with the refresh cookie. The server checks Redis — if the refresh token is still valid, it issues a new 15-minute access JWT.

This solves the revocation problem. If an account is compromised, the administrator deletes the refresh token from Redis. The attacker retains access for at most 15 minutes. Once the access token expires and the refresh handshake fails, the session is dead.

1. Refresh Token Rotation (RTR) and Family Tracking

To further protect against stolen refresh tokens, enterprise systems implement Refresh Token Rotation: the server issues a brand-new refresh token on every single refresh operation, maintaining a token family tree that maps the chain of issued tokens.

If an attacker steals a refresh token and uses it, the server issues a new one to the attacker. Minutes later, when the legitimate user tries to use their cached (now-stale) refresh token, the server detects a Double-Use Violation — this token has already been consumed.

The server immediately flags the entire token family as compromised, deletes every token in the chain from Redis, and forces both the legitimate user and the attacker to re-authenticate. The breach is contained instantly.

2. Distributed Revocation with Bloom Filters

For high-security systems (banking, medical, industrial control), even a 15-minute unrevoked window is too wide. Access tokens must be checked against a revocation list on every request — but doing a database lookup on every request defeats the purpose of stateless tokens.

The solution is a Bloom Filter: a space-efficient, probabilistic data structure that sits in front of the database. It can tell you with certainty that a token is not revoked (a guaranteed negative), or that it might be revoked (a possible positive requiring a database check). It can never falsely declare a revoked token to be valid.

In practice, the vast majority of requests carry valid (non-revoked) tokens. The Bloom filter identifies these instantly with zero database calls, and only triggers a Redis lookup for the rare token that matches the revocation set. This achieves near-zero latency for legitimate traffic while catching every revoked token.

VII. Federated Identity: OAuth 2.0 & OpenID Connect

In distributed systems, clients often need to authorize third-party applications to access resources without sharing their passwords. This is where OAuth 2.0 and OpenID Connect come in.

1. OAuth 2.0

Formalized in RFC 6749, OAuth 2.0 is an authorization framework for delegating access. Instead of sharing credentials with a third-party app, the user authenticates with a centralized Identity Provider. The provider issues a scoped, temporary Access Token to the third-party app, granting limited access to specific resources.

2. Authorization Code Flow with PKCE

Single-Page Applications and mobile apps are vulnerable to Authorization Code Interception, where a malicious app intercepts the temporary authorization code in a browser redirect. PKCE (Proof Key for Code Exchange, RFC 7636) prevents this.

The protocol works in five steps:

  1. The client generates a random string called the Code Verifier.
  2. It hashes the verifier with SHA-256 to produce a Code Challenge.
  3. The client redirects the user to the authorization server, sending the Code Challenge (but not the verifier) in the request.
  4. After login, the server redirects back with a short-lived authorization code. The client exchanges this code for tokens in a direct server-to-server call, sending the original Code Verifier in the request body.
  5. The authorization server hashes the submitted verifier and checks it matches the challenge from step 3. If it matches, the client is proven to be the same one that initiated the login — interception of the authorization code alone is useless without the verifier.

3. OpenID Connect (OIDC)

OAuth 2.0 handles authorization (what you can access) but not identity (who you are). OpenID Connect adds an authentication layer on top, mandating a signed ID Token containing standardized identity claims: user ID (sub), issuer (iss), audience (aud), email, and profile data. It also standardizes a /userinfo endpoint for retrieving profile details. OIDC is the backbone of modern Single Sign-On (SSO) systems.

4. Machine-to-Machine (M2M) Integrations

When two servers communicate directly (e.g., a billing service calling a payment API), browser-based OAuth flows are unusable. These systems use API Key Authentication or the OAuth Client Credentials Grant.

Production-grade API keys follow three rules:

  • Prefix Identifiers: Keys must be prefixed with recognizable text (e.g., sk_live_, pk_test_). This lets automated secret-scanning bots detect leaked credentials in code repositories.
  • Hashing at Rest: Never store raw API keys in the database. Store the SHA-256 hash. When a key arrives, hash it locally and compare against the stored hash. If the database leaks, attackers get only irreversible hashes.
  • Constant-Time Comparison: Never use standard string equality (==) to compare keys. Standard comparison exits early on the first mismatched character, leaking timing information that an attacker can exploit character by character (a Timing Attack). Always use a constant-time comparison function that checks every byte regardless of matches.

VIII. Ancient Indian Guild Administration: The Shreni Ledger

The challenge of proving identity and delegating authority across distances without real-time lookups is not a modern one. One of the most brilliant ancient solutions appeared during the Mauryan and Gupta eras in India, through the governance of the Shrenis — autonomous merchant and artisan guilds.⁴

Shrenis operated as self-governing units with their own judicial systems and mints. As merchants traveled the vast trade corridors of the Uttarapatha (Grand Trunk Road network), they crossed borders and royal toll checkpoints (Ghatikadvara). At each checkpoint, a merchant had to prove their guild membership and their authorization to receive tax exemptions.

The gatekeepers could not send a runner five hundred miles to query the guild-chief's central registry. Animal-based communication latency made stateful lookups physically impossible.

The Shrenis solved this with a Stateless Token System using stamped, clay-sealed emblems called Mudras. When a merchant departed, the guild-chief (Jyesthaka) pressed their unique master signet into wet clay, which was then baked hard. The clay carried iconographic symbols representing the guild, the merchant's rank, and the expiration season. This was a physical JWT — the clay was the payload, and the master-carved imprint was the cryptographic signature.

Plate XXII: The Ancient Indian Shreni Emissary Token Verification Flow

At the toll house, the gatekeeper performed a Stateless Verification. They held a local replica of the official guild signet (the public key). They aligned the merchant's clay seal against it and checked three things:

  • Identity: The emissary represented a verified Shreni.
  • Permissions: The iconographic markings entitled this merchant to specific privileges (e.g., buying silk without municipal tax).
  • Liveness: The stamped trade season was checked against the current season. An expired token was rejected.

Merchant (Sarthavaha) User Agent (browser client holding credentials).

This parallel proves that the engineering trade-offs of cloud networks — centralization versus verification latency — are structural invariants of administrative systems. Whether handling baked clay in 300 CE or parsed base64 strings today, delegation of authority obeys the same immutable laws.

"Authentication is not a passive detail. It is the core boundary that keeps a system secure. Secure the keys. Validate the signatures. And protect the sanctuary of your data."

If a system relies on stateless tokens without managing expiration and revocation, it behaves like Dushyanta's court — a lost ring means total administrative failure. By understanding JWT signatures, securing cookies with strict flags, and deploying multi-token architectures, engineers ensure their servers remain resilient.
