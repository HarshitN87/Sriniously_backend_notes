Chapter the Ninth

Authentication, Authorization & Identity Protocol

"Authentication is the administrative ledger that answers the question: Who are you? Authorization is the sovereign permit that dictates: What are you allowed to do?"
♦ ✦ ♦

Imagine a traveler arriving at the border of the Venetian Republic in the late sixteenth century. The state, intensely paranoid about plague, spies, and Turkish infiltration, maintains a strict two-stage security protocol.

First, the traveler presents a heavy parchment bearing a wax seal from their home parish, confirming their birth name, their physical height, and the color of their eyes. The border guard inspects the wax seal, looks at the traveler’s face, and registers their identity in a leather-bound book. This answers the first administrative question: Who are you? We call this Authentication.

Second, once inside the territory of Venice, the traveler decides they want to visit the state mint—the Zecca—where physical silver and gold are stamped into coins.

When they reach the gate of the Zecca, the guards do not care about the traveler’s birth record or eye color. They do not care if the traveler has been authenticated as "Johann of Münster." They demand a highly different document: a specific, signed permit from the Council of Ten granting visitor clearance to the mint floor. This answers the secondary, distinct question: What are you allowed to do? We call this Authorization.

If Johann presents his birth certificate at the Zecca, the guards will turn him away. If Johann presents a stolen visa that authorizes "Visitor Access," but doesn't have his birth record, they will also turn him away.

Every secure system must implement both of these gates. And in modern backend engineering, we must build them while operating over a network protocol—HTTP—that has no memory whatsoever of the past, forcing us to invent complex cryptographic and administrative mechanisms to maintain the illusion of state across time.

This identity problem is not a software design accident. It is a fundamental property of distributed systems across every scale of physics and history.

We see it in the history of the early Catholic Church's letters of indulgence, where local priests issued signed certificates claiming that a parishioner had performed enough moral penance to offset their sins—a stateless token representing moral credits that could be carried to any parish in Europe. What happened when the local bishop had no way to verify if the certificate's signature was forged?

We see it in the immunological behavior of the human body, where every cell in your organs exposes a specific protein marker called MHC-I on its outer surface. This is a stateless authentication token that tells patrolling white blood cells: "I belong to this body; do not eat me." If a cell is infected by a virus, it stops exposing the marker, and the immune system immediately executes a delete-resource command on the cell.

We even see it in the structure of the classical medieval guild system, where an apprentice spent seven years learning a trade (authentication) before being granted a master’s charter (authorization) to open a workshop, which they had to hang outside their door to prevent the town bailiff from throwing them into debtors' prison for illegal commerce.

I. The Great Memory Crisis: Stateful Sessions

HTTP is a stateless protocol. It treats every single request as a completely fresh transaction.

If a user logs in by submitting their password on Request 1, and then clicks "View Account Profile" on Request 2, the server has already completely forgotten who they are.

In the early days of the web, engineers solved this memory problem by inventing Stateful Sessions.

When a user submits their login form, the server validates their password against the database. If it matches, the server allocates a small block of memory in its active RAM heap or a local file descriptor, stores the user's profile details inside it, and assigns a unique, random string to identify this block—what we call a Session ID: 

session_id = "9283" -> {"userId": 42, "role": "Visitor", "loggedIn": true}

The server sends this Session ID back to the client inside a `Set-Cookie` response header. The browser, behaving according to standard web rules, automatically stores this cookie and attaches it to the request headers of every subsequent request to that domain.

When Request 2 arrives, the server reads the Session ID from the cookie, searches its active RAM heap for a matching block, restores the user state, and processes the request.

This was a simple, clean model that worked beautifully for the early web.

But as the web grew dynamically, stateful sessions hit a massive Scalability Bottleneck.

Suppose your application grows from ten users to one million users. To handle the read volume, you deploy your application across ten independent servers sitting behind a load balancer.

A user logs in. Their request lands on Server A. Server A allocates the session memory block in its local RAM heap and returns Session ID "9283".

Two seconds later, the user clicks a button. The load balancer, routing traffic based on server utilization, routes this second request to Server B.

Server B reads Session ID "9283" from the cookie. It searches its local RAM heap.

It finds nothing.

Because Server A and Server B do not share the same memory heap, Server B has no idea who this user is. It rejects the request as unauthorized, and the user is suddenly, inexplicably logged out of their account mid-transaction.

To solve this, developers had to build centralized session databases (usually using Redis) or enforce "Sticky Sessions" at the load balancer level (routing all requests from a specific browser to the exact same physical server node).

But centralized session databases add network latency to every single incoming request and represent a highly dangerous single point of failure: if your Redis session cache goes down, your entire system locks up and no user in the world can log in. Sticky sessions completely defeat the purpose of horizontal autoscaling, leaving one server overloaded during a traffic spike while the other nine sit idle.

We had built an identity system that was structurally dependent on server memory. To escape this prison, we had to invent a stateless covenant.

II. The Stateless Covenant: JSON Web Tokens (JWT)

The core breakthrough of the stateless revolution was a simple, radical shift in administrative responsibility: Instead of the server storing user state in its memory, the server serializes the user state, signs it, and hands it to the client to hold.

The client becomes the archivist of their own identity.

We call this standard a JSON Web Token (JWT).

A JWT is a flat ASCII string divided into three distinct zones separated by dots:

Plate XIX: Stateful Sessions vs. Stateless JWTs

Let us look at what sits inside these three zones:

1. The Header: A JSON block containing token metadata, encoded in Base64URL. It specifies the algorithm used to sign the token (e.g. `HS256` or `RS256`) and the token type: 

{"alg": "HS256", "typ": "JWT"}

2. The Payload: The data claims. It contains the actual user details—like their user ID (`sub`), their roles (`isAdmin: true`), the registration date, and the token's expiration timestamp (`exp`).

Because the payload is merely encoded in Base64URL and *not* encrypted, anyone can decode the payload and read the user claims. You must never store sensitive secrets—like passwords or database connection details—inside a JWT payload.

3. The Signature: The cryptographic seal. It is computed by taking the Base64-encoded Header, joining it with the Base64-encoded Payload via a dot, and hashing them together with a secret key known *only* to the server: 

HMACSHA256(Base64(Header) + "." + Base64(Payload), secretKey)

When a client requests a protected API route, it sends the JWT inside the `Authorization` header as a Bearer token: 

Authorization: Bearer header.payload.signature

When the server receives this token, it doesn't query a database. It doesn't look up a Redis cache.

It takes the incoming Header and Payload, hashes them together with its internal secret key, and compares the resulting hash with the incoming Signature.

If the signatures match, the server knows mathematically that this token was issued by its own code, that the user is who they claim to be, and that no malicious actor has modified the roles or expiration dates in the payload.

It is a beautiful, self-verifying, stateless passport. It achieves infinite scale because Server A and Server B only need to share the same secret key to authenticate millions of concurrent requests.

III. The Revocation Nightmare

But wait.

If the server does not store active sessions in its memory, what happens when a user wants to log out?

If a user clicks "Log Out" on their dashboard, the client application can delete the token from its local memory. But that is merely a client-side convenience. The physical token still exists. It is floating through the internet, and if an attacker managed to steal that token five minutes before the user logged out, the token remains cryptographically valid.

The server cannot revoke it.

If the attacker sends a request carrying that stolen token, the server will recalculate the signature, see that it matches, check that the expiration date has not been reached, and happily grant the attacker access to the database. The user has logged out, but the system is still allowing their account to be looted.

This is the tragic paradox of statelessness: By removing state from the server to gain scalability, we have discarded administrative control over our own tokens.

If a user’s phone is stolen, or if an administrator wants to force all users to log out to patch a security leak, you are powerless to stop the ongoing sessions until the tokens physically expire. If you set the expiration time to one week to avoid forcing users to log in every ten minutes, you have built a one-week security vulnerability.

To solve this, modern backends implement a Hybrid Token Protocol.

Instead of issuing a single, long-lived token, we split the identity passport into two distinct, sequential components:

  • The Access Token: A stateless, short-lived JWT (typically expires in 15 minutes). It is carried in RAM to authorize fast, high-volume API requests. If it is stolen, the attacker's window of opportunity is limited to a maximum of 15 minutes.
  • The Refresh Token: A stateful, long-lived token (expires in 7 days). It is stored in a database (like Redis) and sent inside a secure cookie. It has a single, narrow purpose: to request a new Access Token when the short-lived one expires.

When the Access Token expires, the client sends the Refresh Token to a `/refresh` endpoint. The server looks up the Refresh Token in its Redis database.

If the token is valid and has not been revoked, the server issues a brand-new 15-minute Access Token. If an administrator wants to terminate a compromised session, they simply delete the Refresh Token from the Redis database. The very next time the client attempts to refresh their session, the server rejects it, and the user's active session is terminated within 15 minutes.

We have bought 99% of the horizontal scalability of stateless tokens at the cost of running a single database query once every 15 minutes instead of on every request. It is a pragmatically optimal compromise.

IV. The Fortress of Cookies: XSS vs. CSRF

If you store your JWTs inside the browser's `localStorage` or `sessionStorage`, you are leaving your identity credentials completely unprotected on the street.

Any malicious JavaScript code running in the browser—whether introduced via a compromised third-party library, an advertising script, or a dynamic blog comment—can read `document.localStorage`.

This is a Cross-Site Scripting (XSS) attack. If an attacker injects a single script line, they can steal every token stored on your user's hard drive and transmit them to their own servers.

To protect our tokens, we must store them inside Secure HTTP-Only Cookies.

When a cookie is configured with specific administrative flags, the browser places it inside a secure container that is completely invisible to JavaScript:

  1. `HttpOnly`: This flag tells the browser: "Never allow any JavaScript script to read this cookie." Even if a hacker successfully executes an XSS attack on your page, their scripts cannot access the cookie. The token remains safe inside the browser's secure memory array.
  2. `Secure`: This flag forces the browser to only transmit the cookie over encrypted HTTPS connections. It prevents network eavesdroppers from intercepting the cookie on public Wi-Fi networks.
  3. `SameSite=Lax/Strict`: This flag blocks Cross-Site Request Forgery (CSRF) attacks.

A CSRF attack is a form of administrative identity fraud.

Suppose a user is logged into their bank at `bank.com`. Without logging out, they navigate to a malicious forum site, `evil.com`.

`evil.com` contains a hidden image tag: 

<img src="https://bank.com/api/transfer?amount=1000&to=evil_hacker" />

When the browser parses this tag, it attempts to fetch the image. Because browsers automatically attach cookies on every request to a target domain, the browser will attach the user's `bank.com` session cookie to this request!

The bank's server receives the request, parses the valid cookie, and transfers $1,000 to the hacker without the user's knowledge.

The `SameSite=Lax` flag blocks this vulnerability by telling the browser: "Only attach this cookie if the request originated from the exact same domain." If the request to `bank.com` originated from `evil.com`, the browser will strip the cookie before sending it, keeping the user’s account safe.

V. OAuth 2.0 Delegation Flow

Suppose you are using a web service that prints physical photo albums. The service wants to access the photos stored inside your Google Photos account.

In the early, naive days of the web, the print service would simply show a login window asking for your raw Google username and password.

This was a catastrophic security pattern. By typing your master password into a third-party application, you granted them total control over your digital identity. They could read your personal emails, delete your calendar, lock you out of your account, and you had no way to restrict their access or revoke their privileges without changing your password entirely.

To solve this, the industry negotiated the OAuth 2.0 delegation protocol.

OAuth 2.0 is an authorization framework designed to allow a third-party application to obtain restricted, temporary access to a resource server without ever seeing the user's password.

By doing this, Google Photos never sees your print shop account details, and the print shop never sees your Google password. It receives a temporary, restricted Access Token that only grants permission to read photos for two hours.

But wait.

OAuth 2.0 was designed strictly as an Authorization delegation tool.

It didn't provide a standard way to verify *who* the user was (Authentication). In the early days of OAuth, developer teams had to write custom, ad-hoc API calls to endpoints like `/me` to fetch usernames and email addresses, parsing dynamic JSON payloads that changed depending on whether they were talking to Google, Facebook, or GitHub.

To solve this, the industry built a standardized identity layer on top of OAuth 2.0, which we call OpenID Connect (OIDC).

OIDC introduces a highly specific, standardized token called the ID Token (`id_token`). Unlike the Access Token—which is an opaque string meant only for the resource server to read—the ID Token is a signed JSON Web Token (JWT) specifically formatted to tell the client application the user's name, email, avatar, and expiration details in a standardized format.

OIDC turned OAuth 2.0 into the universal authentication protocol of the modern web.

VI. The New Pathology: Neo-Feudal Corporate Monopolies

But the transition to OIDC and "Social Login" has created a new, deeply worrying structural pathology: The corporate neo-feudalism of the web.

In the early days of the internet, authentication was decentralized. Every small forum, blog, and website managed its own local user table. You had ten different usernames and passwords for ten different sites.

While this was annoying for users and insecure (because people reused the same simple password across all ten sites), it represented a highly resilient, distributed power structure. If one forum went out of business or banned your account, your other nine digital identities remained completely unaffected.

But today, almost every modern website offers a single, prominent button: "Continue with Google" or "Log in with Apple".

We have built a system of authentication feudalism.

We have outsourced the absolute core of our digital sovereign identities to three massive corporate tech monopolies. Google, Apple, and Facebook act as the digital barons of the realm. Every small website is a tenant farmer renting identity credentials from these barons' castles.

Consider what happens if one of these automated corporate systems goes wrong.

Suppose an automated Google policy bot flags your Gmail account for an alleged violation of terms—perhaps because you shared a family medical photograph that was mistakenly flagged by a computer vision model as contraband.

Your Google account is instantly, unilaterally terminated.

But because you used "Continue with Google" to authenticate across the entire web, you are not just locked out of your email. You are instantly, catastrophically locked out of your online banking, your university dashboard, your home security app, your digital passport records, and your smart refrigerator.

You have experienced a form of digital exile, excommunicated from the modern world by a single automated script, with no human support line to call.

This is the new security pathology: in our pursuit of convenience, safety, and frictionless scale, we have built a centralized, fragile architecture of digital feudalism where our very right to exist online is subject to the automated whims of three global database tables.

VII. Symmetrical Authentication Matrix

Let us synthesize our architectural choices to see how they map to actual development requirements:

Method Identity Format Statefulness Best Use Case
Sessions Random session ID cookie Stateful (RAM/Redis) Monoliths, high security, internal portals
JWT Base64 cryptographically signed string Stateless High-volume, distributed microservices
Hybrid Access Token (JWT) + Refresh Cookie Hybrid (Fast lookup + revocable) SPA web apps, mobile backends
API Keys Static secrets in headers Stateful (DB comparison) Machine-to-Machine external client queries

What is the final lesson here?

Do not look at identity protocols as simple administrative boilerplate to be outsourced or forgotten.

They are the physical boundary gates of your system’s sovereign power.

If your gates are too loose, your city is looted. If your gates are too tight, your economy locks up.

And if you outsource your gates entirely to a foreign baron's army, do not be surprised when they decide to close the highway without warning. Build your gates with care. Understand their cryptographic physics. And keep the keys close.
